# M10: guided movement and a calmer camera

Owner: builder B (own worktree, own task list `docs/TASKS-B.md`; see X00). User request (2026-10-26): "consider slowing the camera and limiting the freedom the player has to move around. We may be able to make puzzles more interesting as well as improve our graphics performance by limiting how much the player can move about. Review how other similar games have handled this and implement a solution." And: no approval needed; the F key is the correction mechanism, so **every amount below is a setting** and the pause menu has a way back to free roam.

## Research: how similar games handle it

The web search was thin (design talks on soft boundaries are mostly not indexed), so what is sourced is marked; the rest is from the reviewer's knowledge of the games and should be treated as design reasoning, not citations.

- **Myst and Riven (originals): node-based.** The player stands at fixed views and turns, so the designers curate every image and know where the player is. Jonathan Blow, comparing it with free 3D, says full 3D loses that: you do not know where the player comes from. (Sourced: PlayStation Blog / PSLS "Dev on Dev" interview of the creators of The Witness and Obduction; Rand Miller there describes the same problem: path guidance that was handled per frame in Riven has to be handled at a different scale in real-time 3D.)
- **Obduction (Cyan): both.** It shipped with a node-warp system (predefined markers) *and* free walking; the hands-on review found the warp-only version felt limiting at first, which is why free roam was added. (Sourced: Road to VR hands-on.) Lesson: pure nodes lose spatial continuity; the player wants to *walk*.
- **Myst (2021): free roam only.** Reviewers noted there is no classic node mode and that positioning yourself for puzzles became fiddlier than clicking a node. (Sourced: reviews at TrueAchievements / PC Gamer / Wccftech.) Lesson: free roam needs help to put the player where the puzzle wants them.
- **The Witness (Thekla):** open island, but the terrain does the limiting (cliffs, walls, water, hedges), walking is slow, running is a held key, and puzzle panels sit on sight lines from the route. (Reviewer knowledge; the search confirmed only that panels are on eye-level, recognisable surfaces and clustered when teaching a rule.)
- **Firewatch, Gone Home, Dear Esther, Eastshade (reviewer knowledge, not searched):** all walk slowly, none has a run you need, and the boundaries are diegetic: a rock wall, a fence, dense undergrowth, a drop, rather than an invisible wall; the route is a gentle funnel with wide side areas. The player rarely meets a hard stop because the soft cues (slope, density, fog) change their mind first.
- **Camera feel (sourced as anecdotes from developer forums):** speed-dependent smoothing keeps small precise movements and tames flicks; heavy smoothing hurts precision; hidden hard caps on turn speed are widely reported as a bug (a slow mouse "barely turns"). Everything should be exposed as settings.

**Design that follows (the hybrid the evidence points to):** *free walking inside soft corridors*. Corridors are generous (6 to 12 m wide, plazas 20 m round the landmarks), so the player never feels on rails, but the reachable world is a small, known region. That gives us: composition control (stations where a view is designed), puzzle control (sight lines, gating), and graphics budget control (we know every place the eye can be).

## What changes for the player

1. **Slower, calmer look.** Default sensitivity down by about a third; light smoothing (critically damped, about 70 ms); a soft angular-speed limit on flicks (tanh compression above about 180 degrees/s: normal turning is untouched); sensitivity scales with the field of view (looking closer is steadier); pitch limited to +-65 degrees.
2. **Slower walk.** Walk 3.2 to about 2.3 m/s (the island is a few hundred metres across: a minute or two between landmarks). Run (held shift) 6.5 to about 3.6 m/s, a "brisk" pace, not a sprint. Slightly longer ease-in and ease-out. Cadence follows (the step length is a function of speed).
3. **Soft corridors.** Inside a corridor or plaza you move freely. Past its edge, a margin 3 to 5 m wide gently slows you (down to 25%) and bends your motion back toward the trail; beyond the margin it is a hard limit (slide along it, as the sea and steep slopes already do). Nothing says "invisible wall": the cues are slowing, the soft steering, and (see M10.4) a quiet closing-in of the ambient.
4. **Vantage stations.** A few marked-by-design spots on the trails where a view is composed (the stones from the causeway end, the lighthouse from the dock, the pool from the altar path). Near one, looking toward its target, the camera gets a light framing assist (a slow pull of a few degrees). Puzzles are built on them, starting with the stones.
5. **A way out.** Pause menu: "Guidance: Guided / Loose / Free roam" (Loose = corridors 1.6x wider, margin 2x). Default Guided. `--freeroam` for tools. The F key records the mode in its context so the user's notes say which they were using.

## Consumers on the graphics side (builder A; listed in G10 stream 3)

## Risks
- **Soft-locks** are the real danger. Hence M10.6 and the fuzz test; the Free roam setting is the user's escape; the F-key context shows the mode.
- **Feeling policed.** If the first playtest says so, widen (Loose) before removing: corridors are authored data, not code.
- **Slow walk and the S02 story:** the Phase 1 plan assumes the fork returns the player to Varrow; at 2.3 m/s a long walk back is dull. The corridor network should keep the key repeated routes short (dock to lighthouse about 60 s at most); M10.3 checks this and reports the times.
